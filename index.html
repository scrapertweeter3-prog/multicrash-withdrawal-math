How many sequence risks does a fifty year retirement buy?

The guides to surviving a bad first decade assume there is only one of it. A fifty year retirement statistically buys more: brutal stretches have arrived roughly twice a century in market history, so a plan built at 35 should expect two or three of them, not one. Price what they cost. Take $1,000,000, spend $50,000 a year (a five percent withdrawal rate), and earn 7 percent in every year nothing goes wrong. A fifty year run with zero crashes ends near $9.1 million. The same run with one 25 percent crash in year one ends near $1.77 million. One bad year, $7.4 million of difference, and that is the optimistic scenario where it only happens once.

Stack a second episode and the plan is on life support. A crash in year one and another in year twenty one leaves $143,982 at year fifty, which is one bad year from zero. A third episode finishes it: crashes in years one, twenty one, and forty one kill the plan in year forty nine, one year short of the finish line. The mechanics compound against you because undoing a 25 percent loss takes a 33.3 percent gain, so every episode starts the recovery from a hole while $50,000 keeps leaving the account. One sequence is the crisis every guide covers. Three is the one that ends the plan.

This is what the four percent rule actually prices. The classic research tested a thirty year window with room for one bad sequence at the wrong time; it was never a subscription to a bad sequence every fifteen years. A retiree at 35 is not buying one insurance policy but a portfolio of them, and the spread between spending rates is the number of claims it can pay. The same $1,000,000, the same three crashes, at a four percent rate ($40,000 a year) ends with $2,433,661 standing. At five percent it ends in year forty nine. The extra point of spending is not lifestyle. It is the third bad decade you can no longer absorb.
